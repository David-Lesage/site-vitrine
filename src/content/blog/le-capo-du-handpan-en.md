## What a guitarist does with a capo

A guitarist clamps a bar onto the neck and then plays exactly the same shapes as before. The fingers don't change; the key does. Nothing has been relearned — only the starting point has moved.

The electronic handpan, in **<abbr title="The app mode where every chord of the key always has the same place on the pan.">Logic mode</abbr>**, does the same thing. You change the anchor note, and everything you already knew how to play stays playable, in the same place, with the same gestures — in another key.

## The anchor wheel is the handpan's capo

On the electronic handpan, the bar is called the **anchor note**. You pick it on a wheel of twelve petals, one per <abbr title="The smallest step between two neighbouring notes, for example from C to C#.">semitone</abbr>. Touching a petal means clamping the capo somewhere else.

Take the <abbr title="The drawing that links, on the pan, the notes of one chord.">constellation</abbr> of **scale degree I** — the one the app calls **Balance**. Choose the anchor note **C**, then **D**, then **A**, and look at the pan each time.

Look at the copper rings: they are the same positions, joined by the same path, in all three columns. What changes is the name written on them — C, E, G in C; D, F#, A in D; A, C#, E in A. The gestures stay, the key changes: exactly what a capo does.

### Why it holds

It is how Logic mode is built.

A degree's shape is drawn on the pan's **positions** — the notes of the pan, identified by where they sit — and not on the note names. Degree I means "those positions, linked in that order". When you change key, the app recalculates **what each position plays**, without ever touching the drawing.

Put another way: the geometry is fixed on one side, the sounding content on the other. <abbr title="Moving every note by the same distance, higher or lower.">Transposing</abbr> only moves the second. That is why the shape never deforms: nothing in the mechanism can make it move.

## Learning a chord without the note names

And if note names get in your way, you can do without them. In the tool palette, **Notation** section, the **🚫** button ("No note names") clears the names on the pan; the **"Playing order number 1·2·3"** box shows instead the order in which to strike the notes of the chord. In the **Hands & fingering** section, the **✋** button ("Hands in colour") colours each number by the hand that plays it: blue for the left, red for the right.

So you can learn a chord without even knowing the names of its notes. What you need to know comes down to two things: **which key** you're in (the anchor note), and **the order of the chords** in the song. And that order is remembered through colours, by thinking in scale degrees — or as a journey of emotions: **I Balance**, **II Momentum**, **III Adventure**, **IV Relaxation**, **V Tension**, **VI Nostalgia**, **VII Mystery**. A song becomes a path of sensations rather than a list of letters. That is what Logic mode is all about.

## Two very concrete uses

### Accompanying a voice

A singer tells you your <abbr title="The series of notes of a key, arranged from lowest to highest.">scale</abbr> sits too high for her. On an acoustic instrument, the conversation stops there. On an electronic handpan, you drop the anchor by two tones and play **the same piece**, with the same gestures, in the <abbr title="The span of notes, from low to high, that a voice sings comfortably.">vocal range</abbr> where her voice settles.

You haven't transposed a piece: you have transposed the instrument. What you rehearsed still holds.

### Joining other musicians

A session starts, someone calls the key. You set your anchor there — one petal to touch on the wheel, the one shown in [the three-key grid](#grille-trois-tonalites) — and play. No "sorry, I'm in D", no second instrument to unpack: the repertoire you know follows you.

## A clear frame, rather than cognitive chaos

This is where it becomes more than a trick. A Neotone can play just about anything; its possibilities are practically infinite. What reads as a beautiful promise on paper becomes, in practice, what David calls **cognitive chaos**: too many open doors, none of them a place to rest your hand.

Logic mode answers that with a frame. **The same placement of notes by degree in every key.** You learn once where the I, the IV and the V live — and that map stays true everywhere. What you gain isn't power, it's stability: a fixed point inside an instrument that could otherwise be everything at once.

This holds for **all major scales and for <abbr title="The basic minor scale, with no altered note.">natural minor</abbr>**: identical shape, different content.

For **<abbr title="Natural minor with its 7th note raised by a semitone.">harmonic minor</abbr>**, the same: in C minor, degree V, moving from natural to harmonic leaves the same four positions lit and the same path. Only one note changes: the 7th rises a semitone, B♭ becomes B. The variant changes what you hear, not what you see.

## Mutant or Neotone 1: what differs

The **Mutant** plays every major and minor scale. It is what makes the reasoning above complete, with its seven degrees available.

On the **Neotone 1**, the notions of degree and chord apply too — but in a limited way: not all seven degrees are there. The logic is the same, the ground is narrower.

## And on the acoustic side

In acoustic mode, there is a **"Try a different key"** button. It serves something else. It lets you **hear your scale in another key** — every note shifted by the same distance — so you can tell whether that pitch would suit your voice better. It's a preview: as soon as you close it, your scale comes back exactly as it was. Its main use is **choosing an instrument before buying it**.

It is not a capo. Your real acoustic pan hasn't moved by a semitone — and it cannot. On an acoustic instrument, a chord's constellation is worked out from the notes actually present: it speaks about *your* instrument, and it changes from one scale to another. It simply isn't the same promise.

## Going further

Once the key no longer holds you back, the next question becomes: **at which height** to place the chords under a voice. That's the subject of the [vocal layouts](/en/blog/layouts-vocaux-handpan) — Deep, Wide, Bright — which move the octaves without touching the shapes.

And if reading the colours on the pan isn't clear yet, go back to [The two ChromaKeys readings](/en/blog/les-deux-visions-chromakeys): the colour that tells you the note, and the colour that tells you its place in the scale. Those two readings are what make the constellation legible.

Open [Handpan Constellation Studio](/en/handpan-app), switch to Logic mode, and change the anchor while keeping your eye on the shape. It will not move.
